\begin{abstract}
Extensions to the deep Q-network (DQN) are usually evaluated with their own tuned hyperparameters and a handful of
random seeds, so a reported gain may belong to the configuration rather than to the extension. We test four
extensions, namely Double DQN, prioritised experience replay (PER), multi-step returns and Averaged DQN, in a paired
randomised controlled trial on the MinAtar suite. Thirty hyperparameter configurations were sampled from a shared
population, and every configuration was trained with DQN and with each extension, so that each extension is compared
with DQN on exactly the same configuration and seed. The outcome is the area under the evaluation curve, a measure of
sample efficiency. Under one shared training recipe, and in a population whose learning rates mostly lie above the
best rate for DQN, Averaged DQN was the only extension that improved on DQN in more than one game, Double DQN helped
little, and PER and multi-step returns were harmful, which contradicts earlier component studies. Three smaller,
exploratory experiments suggest why. With the training recipe and configuration of an earlier MinAtar study, PER and
multi-step returns matched or beat DQN, as that study reported, which suggests that the harm comes from our recipe
rather than from the implementations. Lowering the learning rate made DQN much stronger and removed most of the
advantage of Averaged DQN, which suggests that Averaged DQN mainly stabilises updates that are too large. The results
also show that evaluations based on one checkpoint, a few configurations or the best configuration of each method can
miss or even reverse these effects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The deep Q-network (DQN) of \citet{mnih2015human} learnt to play Atari 2600 games from raw pixels. It did so by
combining Q-learning with a neural network and two stabilising devices: a replay buffer of past experience and a
target network that is updated only periodically. DQN became the base on which many later methods were built.

Many extensions followed, and each changed one part of DQN. Double DQN changes how the learning target is
computed \citep{vanhasselt2016deep}, prioritised experience replay (PER) changes which past experiences are replayed
\citep{schaul2016prioritized}, multi-step ($n$-step) returns look further ahead before
bootstrapping \citep{hernandezgarcia2019understanding,sutton2018reinforcement}, and Averaged DQN averages several
earlier target networks \citep{anschel2017averaged}. Rainbow combined several such components and found prioritised
replay and multi-step returns to be the most important of them \citep{hessel2018rainbow}.

Each extension was, however, introduced with its own training recipe and evaluated on its own tuned configuration.
Deep reinforcement learning is sensitive to hyperparameters, random seeds and implementation
details \citep{henderson2018deep}, and comparisons often rest on a few runs \citep{agarwal2021deep}. When an extension
beats DQN under one tuned configuration, we therefore cannot tell whether the gain belongs to the extension or to the
configuration.

We address this problem with a randomised controlled trial, following \citet{pretorius2020dropout}. In a medical
trial, patients are drawn from a population and receive treatments; here, the ``patients'' are hyperparameter
configurations drawn at random from a shared population, and the ``treatments'' are the extensions. Every
configuration receives every treatment, so each extension is compared with DQN on exactly the same configuration and
seed. This pairing removes the effect of the configuration from each comparison, while the random sampling lets the
results speak for the population rather than for one hand-picked setting. We use the MinAtar suite
\citep{young2019minatar}, whose small games make hundreds of full training runs affordable on ordinary CPUs.

We ask three research questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{RQ1:} under one shared training recipe and across a population of configurations, does each extension
change sample efficiency relative to DQN?
\item \emph{RQ2:} where our results differ from published ones, why? In particular, do the effects depend on the
training recipe and on the learning rate?
\item \emph{RQ3:} would smaller or more typical evaluations, based on one checkpoint, a few configurations or the best
configuration of each method, have reached the same conclusions?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item A paired trial of four DQN extensions on 30 sampled configurations and four MinAtar games, 600 runs in total.
\item Evidence that, under the shared recipe, only Averaged DQN improves on DQN in more than one game, while PER and
$n$-step returns are harmful.
\item A positive control in which our PER and $n$-step DQN help with the recipe and configuration of an earlier
MinAtar study, which suggests that their harm in the main trial comes from the recipe.
\item Evidence that the learning rate matters as much as any extension, and exploratory evidence that the advantage of
Averaged DQN largely disappears at the smallest learning rate we tested.
\item Evidence that single-checkpoint, small and best-configuration evaluations can miss or reverse these effects.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:background} explains DQN and the four extensions, Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the trial and the
additional experiments, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:related} relates them to earlier
work, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them, Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

This section builds up the algorithms we compare, starting from the reinforcement learning problem and ending with
the four extensions. Each equation is followed by what it means in words.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}

In reinforcement learning, an agent learns by trial and error while interacting with an environment
(Fig.~\ref{fig:agent_env}). At each time step $t$, the agent observes a state $s_t$, chooses an action $a_t$, receives
a reward $r_{t+1}$ and moves to a new state $s_{t+1}$. This interaction is formalised as a Markov decision process, in
which the next state and reward depend only on the current state and action \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}.

The agent's goal is to collect as much reward as possible over time. It therefore maximises the \emph{return},
the discounted sum of future rewards,
\begin{equation}
G_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma r_{t+2}+\gamma^2 r_{t+3}+\dots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1}.
\end{equation}
The discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$ makes a reward received $k$ steps in the future worth $\gamma^k$ of the same
reward received now. With $\gamma=0.99$, as in this work, a reward 100 steps away still counts for about a third of
its value, so the agent plans far ahead.

To choose actions, the agent estimates how good each action is. The action-value function $Q^{\pi}(s,a)$ is the
expected return when the agent takes action $a$ in state $s$ and then follows its policy $\pi$. The best possible
action values, $Q^*(s,a)=\max_\pi Q^{\pi}(s,a)$, satisfy the Bellman optimality equation
\citep{sutton2018reinforcement},
\begin{equation}
Q^*(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\Big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\Big],
\label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
where $s'$ is the next state and the expectation is over the reward and next state. In words, the value of an action
is the reward it brings plus the discounted value of the best action available afterwards. Once $Q^*$ is known, acting
optimally is simple: in each state, choose the action with the largest value.

\subsection{Q-Learning and the Deep Q-Network}
Q-learning \citep{watkins1992q} learns $Q^*$ from experience by turning~\eqref{eq:bellman} into an update. After
observing a transition $(s,a,r,s')$, it computes the \emph{temporal-difference (TD) error}
\begin{equation}
\delta=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a),
\end{equation}
the gap between a one-step estimate of the value, $r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$, and the current estimate $Q(s,a)$. It then
moves $Q(s,a)$ a small step in the direction of $\delta$. Using the agent's own estimate of the next state's value
in place of the true return is called \emph{bootstrapping}.

DQN \citep{mnih2015human} represents $Q$ with a neural network $Q(s,a;\theta)$ with weights $\theta$, so that it can
handle states such as images, which are too many to list in a table. Three ideas make this work. First, transitions
are stored in a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ \citep{lin1992self}, and the network learns from random mini-batches drawn
from it; this breaks the strong correlation between consecutive transitions and lets each transition be reused.
Second, the network is trained to minimise the squared TD error,
\begin{equation}
L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim U(\mathcal{D})}\Big[\big(y-Q(s,a;\theta)\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $U(\mathcal{D})$ means that transitions are drawn uniformly from the buffer. Third, the learning target
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-)
\label{eq:dqn}
\end{equation}
is computed with a separate \emph{target network} whose weights $\theta^-$ are a copy of $\theta$ that is refreshed only
every $C$ steps. Without it, every update would also move the target, and the network would chase its own tail. When
$s'$ ends the episode, there is no future value and the target is simply $y=r$.

The agent explores with an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy: with probability $\varepsilon$ it takes a random action, and
otherwise the action with the largest estimated value. Here $\varepsilon$ starts at 1, so the agent first acts
entirely at random, and is lowered linearly to a small final value.

\subsection{The Four Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}
Each extension below changes exactly one part of DQN: the target, the sampling of the replay buffer, the horizon of
the target, or the target network.

\emph{Double DQN} \citep{vanhasselt2016deep} reduces overestimation. The maximum in~\eqref{eq:dqn} uses the same noisy
estimates both to pick the best next action and to value it. When the estimates contain random errors, the action that
looks best is often one whose value was overestimated, so the maximum of the estimates tends to exceed the true
maximum, and the error is passed on through bootstrapping. Double DQN separates the two roles: the online network
$\theta$ picks the action and the target network $\theta^-$ values it,
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\,Q\big(s',\textstyle\arg\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta);\theta^-\big).
\end{equation}
Because the two networks' errors are partly independent, an action that is overestimated by one is less likely to be
overestimated by the other.

\emph{Prioritised experience replay} \citep{schaul2016prioritized} replays surprising transitions more often. Each
transition $i$ has a priority $p_i=|\delta_i|+\kappa$, its latest absolute TD error plus a small constant $\kappa$
that keeps every transition sampleable. Transition $i$ is drawn with probability
\begin{equation}
P(i)=\frac{p_i^{\alpha}}{\sum_k p_k^{\alpha}}.
\end{equation}
The exponent $\alpha$ sets how strongly the priorities count: $\alpha=0$ gives uniform sampling, as in DQN, and
$\alpha=1$ samples in direct proportion to the TD error. New transitions receive the largest priority seen so far,
so that each is replayed at least once.

Sampling by priority changes the data the network learns from, and therefore the expected gradient. PER corrects this
with importance-sampling weights,
\begin{equation}
w_i=\frac{\big(|\mathcal{D}|\,P(i)\big)^{-\beta}}{\max_{j\in\mathcal{D}}\big(|\mathcal{D}|\,P(j)\big)^{-\beta}},
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{D}|$ is the number of transitions in the buffer, and the maximum in the denominator is attained by
the transition with the lowest priority. Uniform sampling would draw transition $i$ with probability
$1/|\mathcal{D}|$; when $\beta=1$, multiplying its loss by the numerator alone, $(|\mathcal{D}|\,P(i))^{-1}$, exactly
undoes the over- or under-sampling in expectation. The denominator divides every weight by the same number, so that
every weight is at most one; this rescales the expected gradient without changing its direction, so after
normalisation the correction is exact only up to this common factor, which acts like a smaller step size. PER starts with a partial
correction, $\beta=\beta_0<1$, and raises $\beta$ linearly to 1 by the end of training, when an unbiased gradient
matters most \citep{schaul2016prioritized}. Each transition's loss in~\eqref{eq:loss} is multiplied by its weight $w_i$.

\emph{Multi-step returns} \citep{hernandezgarcia2019understanding,sutton2018reinforcement} look $n$ steps ahead before
bootstrapping:
\begin{equation}
y=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1}+\gamma^n\max_{a'}Q(s_{t+n},a';\theta^-).
\end{equation}
The first term is the discounted reward actually observed over the next $n$ steps; the second is the estimated value
of the state reached after them. A reward therefore reaches the value of a state $n$ steps earlier in a single update,
instead of creeping back one step per update. The price is bias: the $n$ observed rewards come from the exploring
behaviour policy, not the greedy policy whose value is being learnt. Like Rainbow \citep{hessel2018rainbow}, we apply
no off-policy correction, so this difference is ignored.

\emph{Averaged DQN} \citep{anschel2017averaged} averages the last $K$ target networks $\theta^-_1,\dots,\theta^-_K$
before taking the maximum:
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q(s',a';\theta^-_k).
\label{eq:avg}
\end{equation}
Each target network makes its own approximation error. Averaging $K$ estimates reduces the variance of their error,
by a factor of $K$ if the errors were uncorrelated and by less when they are correlated, which gives a steadier
target \citep{anschel2017averaged}. The cost is $K$ forward passes per target; the number of gradient updates is the
same as in DQN.

\subsection{Trial Design}
Fig.~\ref{fig:design} summarises the design. The population $\Lambda$ contains every combination of the candidate
values of six shared hyperparameters (Table~\ref{tab:population}). Each of the $N=30$ individuals $\lambda_j$ was drawn
by sampling each hyperparameter independently and uniformly from its candidates with a seeded random generator, and
was given its own training seed, $1337+j$. Because this is a simple random sample, the counts in
Table~\ref{tab:population} are not perfectly balanced.

The candidate values were chosen around the MinAtar DQN baseline. \citet{young2019minatar} scaled DQN's replay
capacity, target-update period, exploration schedule and replay warm-up down by a factor of ten relative to
\citet{mnih2015human}, giving $10^5$, 1000, $10^5$ and 5,000 steps. Each candidate range in Table~\ref{tab:population}
contains the corresponding baseline value, as do the batch size of 32 and the single hidden layer of the baseline. The
learning-rate candidates span the $2.5\times10^{-4}$ used by \citet{mnih2015human} and by the MinAtar baseline, although
that value is not itself one of them. Every run starts by filling the
buffer with 5,000 transitions from uniformly random actions, the baseline's warm-up.

Five arms were trained on every individual: DQN as the control and the four extensions as treatments. Within an
individual, all arms share the configuration, the training seed and the evaluation seeds, so every comparison with
DQN is paired. The extensions' own settings were fixed at published values rather than sampled: $\alpha=0.6$ and
$\beta_0=0.4$ for PER, the values \citet{schaul2016prioritized} chose for its proportional variant; $n=3$ for $n$-step
DQN, as in Rainbow \citep{hessel2018rainbow}; and $K=10$ for Averaged DQN, one of the values tested by
\citet{anschel2017averaged}. PER's priority constant, $\kappa=10^{-6}$, is our choice; it only has to be small
enough not to change the priorities of transitions with non-zero error.

MinAtar \citep{young2019minatar} re-implements five Atari games on a $10\times10$ grid (Fig.~\ref{fig:minatar}). The
agent observes a $10\times10\times n$ binary array with one channel per kind of object: $n=4$ in Breakout and

Atari games while making each run cheap enough for a population of configurations; its authors designed it for
exactly this kind of thorough, reproducible experiment \citep{young2019minatar}. We kept MinAtar's default settings, including
sticky actions with probability 0.1, which repeat the previous action at random and make the games stochastic.

The main trial uses Breakout, Asterix, Freeway and Space Invaders, giving $4\times5\times30=600$ runs. Seaquest was
also run but is reported separately, because our evaluation protocol initially failed on it: in MinAtar's Seaquest,
oxygen only runs down while the submarine is below the surface, so a greedy agent that never dives is never
killed and its episode never ends. The main trial aborted any run whose evaluation episode passed 100,000 steps.
Seaquest jobs started for four of the five arms (the $n$-step DQN job failed at start-up), and this guard stopped all
four. The additional experiments below repeat Seaquest with the evaluation episode capped instead.

\subsection{Agent and Shared Training Recipe}

baseline \citep{young2019minatar}: one convolutional layer of 16 filters followed by one or two hidden layers of 128
units. This is much smaller than the network of \citet{mnih2015human}, which has three convolutional layers of 32, 64
and 64 filters and a hidden layer of 512 units. MinAtar has no frame skipping, so a frame is one environment
step, and each run lasts $5\times10^{6}$ frames, as in the MinAtar baselines.

The recipe, meaning the optimiser and its constants, the loss, the update frequency and the exploration schedule, is
shared by all arms and was fixed before training. It is not the recipe of DQN or of the MinAtar baseline, and
Table~\ref{tab:recipes} shows where it departs from them. Like both, we used RMSProp \citep{tieleman2012rmsprop}, but
with PyTorch's default constants (second-moment decay 0.99, stabilising constant $10^{-8}$, not centred) rather than
theirs (0.95 and 0.01, centred). The difference may matter. RMSProp divides each gradient by the root mean square of
that parameter's recent gradients plus the constant, so with a constant of $10^{-8}$ even a parameter whose gradients
are tiny moves by about the full learning rate, whereas a constant of 0.01 damps such steps. At the same learning rate,
our recipe may therefore take larger effective steps than the baseline, which would fit the finding that the smallest
learning rates did best (Sections~\ref{sec:variation} and~\ref{sec:lowlr}).

We used the plain squared TD error of~\eqref{eq:loss}, without clipping. DQN clipped its error terms, which is
equivalent to the Huber loss, and the MinAtar baseline uses the Huber loss. We chose the squared error because it is
the loss in which DQN and every extension above are written, so that no arm's errors are clipped;
\citet{obandoceron2021revisiting} found the squared loss better than the Huber loss on MinAtar when combined with Adam,
although with RMSProp they found that the Huber loss tended to do better. One gradient step per frame, the same for
every arm, matches the MinAtar baseline; DQN updated once every four agent steps. The discount 0.99 and final
exploration rate 0.1 are those of both. Taken together, RMSProp with these constants, an unclipped squared loss and an
update every frame make a recipe that is shared by all arms but not standard.

The recipe is not tuned for any arm, and that is deliberate. Changing one component under a fixed recipe is what
isolates the component's effect. The price is that the main trial's results describe the components under this
recipe, not in general; the additional experiments measure how much depends on that.

The extensions were implemented as follows. PER stores priorities in a sum tree and draws each mini-batch by
stratified sampling: the total priority is split into equal segments and one transition is drawn from each.
$n$-step DQN stores, for each transition, the discounted sum of the next three rewards and the state three steps later;
at the end of an episode, the shorter remaining sums are stored without a bootstrap term. Averaged DQN stores a copy of
the target network at every target update and averages the last ten copies as in~\eqref{eq:avg}, or all of them while
fewer than ten exist.

\subsection{Evaluation and Primary Outcome}
Every $5\times10^{4}$ frames, training pauses and the current network plays 10 evaluation episodes greedily, that is
with $\varepsilon=0$, so that the evaluation measures what the agent has learnt rather than its exploration. The ten
episodes use the same environment seeds (1000--1009) for every individual, arm and checkpoint, so the arms face exactly
the same game situations. The mean return over the ten episodes, $G_i$ at checkpoint $i$, is one point on the
evaluation curve, giving 100 points per run.

The primary outcome is the area under this curve (AUC), computed with the trapezoidal rule:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}=\sum_{i=1}^{99}\frac{G_i+G_{i+1}}{2}\,\Delta x,\qquad \Delta x=5\times10^{4}\ \text{frames}.
\end{equation}
Each term is the area of a strip between two neighbouring checkpoints: its width is the $5\times10^4$ frames between them
and its height is the average of their returns. We report the AUC in units of $10^{6}$ (return $\times$ frames).
Dividing the AUC by the $4.95\times10^{6}$ frames it spans gives the average evaluation return over training, so a high
AUC requires both learning fast and reaching a high level. That is why we use it as our measure of sample efficiency.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}\label{sec:stats}
The unit of analysis is the individual. The ten evaluation episodes at a checkpoint, and the 100 checkpoints of a run,
all measure the same agent; treating them as independent observations would exaggerate the sample size. Each run
therefore contributes one number, its AUC.

For each game and treatment, we form the 30 paired differences
\begin{equation}
d_j=\mathrm{AUC}_j^{\mathrm{treatment}}-\mathrm{AUC}_j^{\mathrm{DQN}},\qquad j=1,\dots,30,
\end{equation}
one per individual. Pairing pays off whenever a configuration that suits DQN also suits the treatment. The variance of
the mean difference is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\bar d)=\frac{\sigma_T^2+\sigma_C^2-2\rho\,\sigma_T\sigma_C}{N},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_T$ and $\sigma_C$ are the standard deviations of the treatment's and DQN's AUC across individuals and $\rho$
is their correlation. When the configuration drives both arms in the same direction, $\rho$ is large and positive, and
the covariance term cancels much of the variance between configurations that an unpaired comparison would have to
carry.

The primary test is the two-sided paired $t$-test. It asks whether the mean difference $\bar d$ is larger than chance
would produce if the treatment had no effect, using the statistic
\begin{equation}
t=\frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt{N}},
\end{equation}
where $s_d$ is the standard deviation of the 30 differences, so the denominator is the standard error of $\bar d$.
Under the null hypothesis of no mean effect, $t$ follows a $t$-distribution with $N-1=29$ degrees of freedom, and the
$p$-value is the probability of a $|t|$ at least as large as the one observed.

We run 16 such tests, four treatments in four games, and each test has a chance of a false positive. If each test used
the usual threshold of 0.05, the probability of at least one false positive among 16 independent tests would be
$1-0.95^{16}\approx0.56$. Because our conclusions compare effects across games, we treat all 16 tests as one family and
apply the Bonferroni correction \citep{dunn1961multiple}: a test counts as significant only if
$p\le\alpha=0.05/16=0.003125$. The probability of at least one false positive is at most the sum of the 16 individual
probabilities, $16\alpha=0.05$, whether or not the tests are independent. The matching confidence interval for the mean
difference is $\bar d\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,29}\,s_d/\sqrt{N}$, a 99.69\% interval, which we divide by DQN's mean AUC to
express it as a percentage.

We report each effect as a percentage of DQN's mean, $\Delta=100\,\bar d/\overline{\mathrm{AUC}}^{\mathrm{DQN}}$, together
with the number of individuals, out of 30, on which the treatment beat DQN. With no effect, about 15 wins would be
expected.

As our one sensitivity check, we repeat every comparison with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test
\citep{wilcoxon1945individual}. It ranks the absolute differences $|d_j|$ from smallest to largest, sums the ranks of
the positive differences, and asks whether that sum is unusually large or small. Because it uses ranks, a few extreme
configurations cannot dominate it, which complements the $t$-test, whose mean can be pulled by outliers. The check is
needed: the $t$-test assumes that the mean difference is roughly normally distributed, and the Shapiro--Wilk
test \citep{shapiro1965analysis} rejects normality of the differences for 6 of the 16 comparisons. We report both
tests for every comparison, rather than choosing between them with a normality test, because such a pre-test changes
the error rates of the test that follows \citep{rochon2012test}.

All other analyses are exploratory and are reported in the appendices: the ratio scale, Holm's procedure, secondary
endpoints, the reliability of smaller evaluations and a regression on the sampled hyperparameters.

\subsection{Additional Experiments}\label{sec:additional}
The main trial found PER and $n$-step returns harmful, against earlier component studies, and found the smallest
learning rate best for almost every arm (Section~\ref{sec:variation}). We ran three additional experiments to explain
these findings. All of them use the same code, environments and evaluation as the main trial.

\emph{Recipe and positive control.} If our PER and $n$-step DQN are correct, they should help in a setting where they are
known to help. \citet{obandoceron2021revisiting} found multi-step returns to be the most useful addition to DQN on
MinAtar. Their recipe differs from ours in three ways (Table~\ref{tab:recipes}): the Adam optimiser
\citep{kingma2015adam} (stabilising constant $3.125\times10^{-4}$), one gradient step every four frames, and a final
exploration rate of 0.01; like ours, it uses the squared loss. We
trained DQN, PER and $n$-step DQN on Asterix and Freeway in two settings. The first applies their recipe to the seven
individuals of our population with learning rate $10^{-4}$, keeping each individual's other hyperparameters. The second
uses their published DQN configuration (learning rate $2.5\times10^{-4}$, replay capacity $10^5$, batch 32, target
period 1000, exploration decay over 250,000 frames, warm-up 1,000) with five seeds. Their paper does not state the
number of hidden layers for MinAtar, so we used one, as in the MinAtar baseline. To check DQN itself, the second
setting was also run for DQN in Breakout and Space Invaders.

\emph{Lower learning rates.} Because the smallest learning rate in the population, $10^{-4}$, was the best for almost every
arm, we trained all five arms on the same seven individuals at $3\times10^{-5}$ and $10^{-5}$, under the shared recipe,
in all four games. These seven are not a random sample of the population: they were selected because their learning
rate was $10^{-4}$, and only their learning rate was changed.

\emph{Seaquest with capped evaluation.} We trained all five arms on all 30 individuals in Seaquest, with each
evaluation episode stopped at 100,000 steps and scored with the reward collected so far.

These experiments were designed after the main results were known, and the first two use only five or seven
configurations. We therefore treat them as exploratory: we report effects and win counts, without significance tests,
and read their conclusions as suggestions rather than findings.
Seaquest has all 30 individuals, so it receives the primary analysis as its own family of four tests, with threshold
$0.05/4=0.0125$.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
The agents were implemented in PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} (version 2.13) with the
Gymnasium \citep{towers2024gymnasium} interface to MinAtar (version 1.0.15) and trained on CPUs on the cluster of the
University of the Witwatersrand. Before every cluster job, unit tests and analytic checks verify each arm's targets and
replay. Among them, PER's sampling probabilities and importance weights are compared with analytic values, $n$-step
returns with an independent episode-level computation, and $n$-step DQN with $n=1$ is checked to reproduce DQN's network
weights exactly through the full training loop.

\subsection{RQ3: Would Smaller Evaluations Have Agreed?}\label{sec:reliability}
Appendix~\ref{app:reliability} repeats the main comparisons as smaller or more typical studies would have run them, and
the answer is that they would often have reached weaker or different conclusions.

Single-checkpoint endpoints, the mean of the last ten evaluations or the final evaluation alone, agreed in direction
with all nine findings but detected fewer of them. They have less power than the AUC, not different conclusions.

Trials with a few configurations usually got the direction right but seldom reached significance. A small study that
finds no effect therefore says little about whether an effect exists.

Comparing the best configuration of each arm with the best of DQN, as separately tuned studies effectively do, reversed
two findings: it would have reported $n$-step DQN as better than DQN in Space Invaders and PER as better in Freeway.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

This section describes the studies closest to ours and explains how our work differs from each.

\emph{Component studies.} \citet{hessel2018rainbow} combined six extensions into Rainbow and removed each in turn on 57
Atari games; removing prioritised replay or multi-step returns hurt most. Their ablation removes one component from a
tuned combination, with one configuration. We instead add one component to DQN, over a random population of
configurations, and find that the same two components can hurt DQN under a different recipe.

\citet{obandoceron2021revisiting} repeated Rainbow's additive and ablative studies on small environments, including
MinAtar, with Adam and a learning rate swept for each component, and found multi-step returns to be the most useful
addition to DQN on MinAtar. Their study varies the component and the learning rate with the rest fixed; ours varies six
hyperparameters at random and pairs every comparison. Our positive control reproduces their finding under their recipe,
and our main trial shows that it does not carry over to ours.

\citet{fedus2020revisiting} showed that uncorrected $n$-step returns benefit from a larger replay capacity, whereas DQN
does not. Their experiment varies the replay setting directly; in our population the replay capacity is one of six
sampled factors, and our subgroups were too small to show its effect on $n$-step DQN.

\citet{fujimoto2020equivalence} showed that a loss evaluated on prioritised samples has the same expected gradient as a
different loss on uniform samples, so the effect of PER depends on the loss it is combined with. This is a theoretical
result; our trial is an empirical test of PER with one loss and one optimiser, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} uses
the equivalence to interpret PER's deficit.

\citet{anschel2017averaged} introduced Averaged DQN and showed on Atari that it reduces the variance of the target and
stabilises learning. They compared it with DQN on fixed configurations. Our trial confirms its benefit across a
population of configurations, and our exploratory runs suggest that the benefit shrinks when the learning rate is
small.

\emph{Evaluation methodology.} \citet{henderson2018deep} showed that hyperparameters, seeds, environments and codebases
can each change reported results substantially. We take this as the motivation for our design: rather than fixing these
factors, we sample the hyperparameters and pair every comparison on them.

\citet{machado2018revisiting} revisited the evaluation protocols of the Arcade Learning Environment and introduced sticky
actions. We follow their recommendation through MinAtar's sticky actions, and our Seaquest results show how an
evaluation protocol choice, aborting versus capping long episodes, can decide whether a game can be evaluated at all.

\citet{colas2018many} related the number of seeds to the error rates of statistical tests, and \citet{agarwal2021deep}
showed that point estimates over few runs can mislead and recommended interval estimates and the probability of
improvement. Our win counts are a paired version of their probability of improvement, and our subsampling analysis
measures directly how often small trials miss real effects.

\citet{jordan2020evaluating} argued that tuning hyperparameters and then reporting performance answers the wrong
question, and evaluated algorithms whose hyperparameters are drawn at random from a fixed distribution in every trial.
\citet{patterson2024empirical} give a broader guide to empirical design in reinforcement learning. We share the idea of
sampling hyperparameters from a distribution, but give every arm the same draws, which turns each comparison into a
paired one.

\citet{pretorius2020dropout} brought the randomised controlled trial to deep learning, sampling network designs from a
population and training identical copies under every intervention. We apply their design to reinforcement learning,
where training is noisier and each run far more expensive, and to algorithmic extensions rather than network
initialisation.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Which Extensions Help Under the Shared Recipe?}
Averaged DQN was the only extension that improved on DQN in more than one game, and it did so on most configurations in
each of those games. Double DQN's single gain was fragile, and PER and $n$-step returns were harmful. Under one fixed
recipe and a random population of configurations, most of which use too large a learning rate for DQN, the
extensions' published benefits therefore appeared only for Averaged DQN. These are findings about this population and
this recipe, not about the extensions at their best settings.

\subsection{RQ2: Why Do Our Results Differ from Published Ones?}
Each difference from published results has an explanation that our runs are consistent with. The experiments behind
these explanations are small and exploratory, so we offer them as hypotheses that the data suggest, not as
established causes.

Our DQN is weaker on average than the published MinAtar DQN because most of our configurations use too large a
learning rate. At a learning rate of $10^{-5}$, or with a published configuration, it exceeds the published level in
every game except Seaquest (Section~\ref{sec:sanity}).

PER and $n$-step returns are harmful in our main trial, unlike in Rainbow and in the MinAtar study of
\citet{obandoceron2021revisiting}, and the positive control suggests that the recipe is responsible. With that
study's recipe and configuration, both extensions help, as reported there (Section~\ref{sec:recipe}). Since both
recipes use the squared loss, the difference lies in the optimiser and its constants, the update frequency or the
final exploration rate (Table~\ref{tab:recipes}). Our experiments change these together, so they cannot say which of
them matters.

PER's deficit is not caused by the step size alone. \citet{schaul2016prioritized} lowered the step size for PER because
prioritised samples have larger errors and give larger gradients. A smaller learning rate did remove most of PER's
deficit in Space Invaders and turned it into a gain in Breakout, but PER remained worse than DQN on every
configuration at both smaller rates in Asterix and Freeway (Section~\ref{sec:lowlr}).

The equivalence of \citet{fujimoto2020equivalence} suggests a reason that involves the loss. PER draws transition $i$
with probability proportional to $|\delta_i|^{\alpha}$ and multiplies its loss by a weight proportional to
$(|\mathcal{D}|P(i))^{-\beta}$, so the product is proportional to $|\delta_i|^{\alpha(1-\beta)}$. With the squared loss,
PER's expected gradient is therefore the gradient of a uniformly sampled loss proportional to
$|\delta|^{2+\alpha(1-\beta)}$. With $\alpha=0.6$ and $\beta_0=0.4$, our PER starts out behaving like DQN with a loss of
power 2.36, which weights large errors even more heavily than the squared loss does, and the power falls to two only
as $\beta$ reaches one at the end of training. \citet{schaul2016prioritized} instead combined PER with clipped errors,
for which the power for large errors lies between one and two, and \citet{fujimoto2020equivalence} use this
equivalence to argue that prioritisation suits the Huber loss better than the squared loss. Under our recipe, the
update direction may thus be dominated by the few transitions with the largest errors. The loss cannot be the whole
explanation, because the recipe of \citet{obandoceron2021revisiting} also uses the squared loss and PER helped there
in Asterix; if the loss matters, it matters in combination with the optimiser or the update frequency. Recording the
TD errors and importance weights during training would test this.

$n$-step DQN's deficit depends on both the learning rate and the recipe. At the smallest learning rate it nearly
caught up with DQN in Freeway and overtook it in Breakout and Space Invaders, and under the other recipe it came close to DQN on most
configurations in both games tested. Only in Asterix under the shared recipe did it fail at every learning rate, and
with the published configuration it beat DQN there on every seed. That configuration has a replay capacity ten times
that of six of the seven configurations we retrained, and \citet{fedus2020revisiting} found that uncorrected $n$-step
returns benefit from a larger replay capacity; but the configuration differs in other ways too, and the one retrained
configuration with the larger capacity behaved like the rest, so we cannot attribute the difference to replay capacity.

Averaged DQN's gain agrees with \citet{anschel2017averaged}, who presented it as a way to stabilise learning, and our
learning-rate experiment suggests when this matters: its advantage was large at $10^{-4}$, persisted in Freeway at
$3\times10^{-5}$, and was gone at $10^{-5}$. One possible reason is that averaging ten target networks smooths the
target, which compensates for updates that are too large; when the updates are already small, there is little left
to smooth. We did not measure the variance of the target, so this remains a hypothesis.

Double DQN helped less than in \citet{vanhasselt2016deep}, whose Atari agents showed large overestimation. We did not
record Q-value estimates, so we cannot tell whether overestimation was small in MinAtar or whether reducing it did not
raise the AUC. This was a missed opportunity: logging the mean predicted value of the greedy action on the evaluation
episodes, next to the discounted return actually obtained, would have cost almost nothing and would have tested
Double DQN's mechanism directly. Its gain in Asterix, like that of Averaged DQN, disappeared at smaller learning rates.

Seaquest scores are far below the published ones because of the evaluation protocol. Greedy evaluation lets an agent
that has not learnt to dive and resurface survive at the surface without scoring, while the published curve averages
training episodes, during which exploration moves the submarine. We did not run Seaquest at smaller learning rates or
with the published configuration, so we cannot say how much of the gap these would close.

Together, these results suggest that the effect of an extension depends on the recipe and the learning rate it is
combined with. Averaged DQN and Double DQN appear to help mainly when the updates are too large, in which case a
population dominated by large learning rates flatters them. PER and $n$-step DQN were hurt by the shared recipe. When
the learning rate alone was lowered, $n$-step DQN largely recovered in Breakout, Freeway and Space Invaders, and PER in
Breakout and Space Invaders, but neither recovered in Asterix, and PER did not in Freeway.

\subsection{RQ3: How Reliable Are Smaller Evaluations?}
The single-checkpoint endpoints reached the same conclusions as the AUC but detected fewer effects, so they waste data
rather than mislead. Small trials and best-configuration comparisons are more dangerous: the first often miss real
effects, and the second reversed two of our findings, because the effects depend on the configuration. Pairing over a
random population avoids both problems.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
Each configuration was trained with one seed, so within an individual the effects of the configuration and of the seed
cannot be separated. Both are shared across arms, so the comparisons remain paired.

The main trial's conclusions hold for one shared recipe, RMSProp with PyTorch's default constants, an unclipped
squared loss and one update per frame, which is not the recipe of DQN or of the MinAtar baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:recipes}). They also hold only for a learning-rate range that, as the additional experiments show,
lies above the best rate for DQN in most games. Its effects describe the extensions in this population, not at their
best settings.

The additional experiments are small and exploratory. The recipe experiment covers two games and seven configurations
and changes the optimiser, the update frequency and the exploration rate together. The learning-rate experiment uses
seven configurations that were selected because they had the population's smallest learning rate, so they are not a
random sample. Seaquest was not run at smaller learning rates.

We recorded returns but no internal quantities. Without Q-value estimates, the variance of the target or PER's TD
errors and importance weights, we cannot test the mechanisms by which the extensions are meant to work, and the
explanations in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} remain hypotheses.

The extensions' own settings were fixed rather than sampled. With more compute, the population could also randomise the
optimiser, the loss, the update frequency, the number of steps $n$ and PER's exponent $\alpha$, so that the trial would
cover the recipe as well as the configuration.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In a paired randomised controlled trial of four DQN extensions over 30 sampled configurations on MinAtar, only Averaged
DQN improved on DQN in more than one game, Double DQN helped little, and PER and multi-step returns were harmful. These
findings hold under the shared recipe and in a population whose learning rates mostly lie above the best rate for DQN.
Smaller, exploratory experiments suggest why they disagree with earlier studies: with an earlier study's recipe and
configuration, PER and multi-step returns helped as reported, and at smaller learning rates DQN became much stronger
while most of Averaged DQN's advantage disappeared. Evaluations based on one checkpoint, few configurations or the best
configuration of each method would have missed or reversed some of these effects. Our results suggest that an
extension's value depends on the recipe it is tested with, and that comparisons should be paired over a population of
configurations that includes the recipe and should record the internal quantities, such as Q-value estimates, that
each extension is meant to change.
