\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Matimu Khosa}

\begin{abstract}
Extensions to the deep Q-network (DQN) are usually evaluated with their own tuned hyperparameters and a handful of
random seeds, so a reported gain may reflect the configuration rather than the extension. We evaluate four
extensions, namely Double DQN, prioritised experience replay, multi-step returns and Averaged DQN, in a paired
randomised controlled trial. Thirty hyperparameter configurations were sampled from a shared population, each with
its own seed, and every configuration was trained with DQN and with each extension on four MinAtar games, giving
600 runs of five million frames. The primary outcome was the area under the greedy evaluation curve, compared with
DQN by paired t-tests with a Bonferroni correction across all sixteen comparisons. Averaged DQN improved on DQN in
Asterix, Freeway and Space Invaders, by 58, 21 and 19 per cent. Double DQN improved only in Space Invaders, by
11 per cent, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test did not confirm this. Prioritised replay was worse in Freeway and
Space Invaders, and multi-step returns were worse in Asterix, Freeway and Space Invaders, by up to 80 per cent. No
extension differed detectably from DQN in Breakout. The sampled learning rate was associated with larger differences
in performance than fifteen of the sixteen treatment effects, and evaluating only the final checkpoint confirmed
three of the nine findings. Under a shared training recipe, Averaged DQN was the only reliable improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The deep Q-network (DQN) of \citet{mnih2015human} learnt to play Atari 2600 games from pixels, using two
stabilising devices: experience replay and a periodically updated target network. Many extensions followed, each
changing one part of the algorithm. Double DQN changes how the bootstrap target is computed \citep{vanhasselt2016deep},
prioritised experience replay (PER) changes which transitions are replayed \citep{schaul2016prioritized}, multi-step
($n$-step) returns lengthen the horizon of the target \citep{hernandezgarcia2019understanding,sutton2018reinforcement},
and Averaged DQN averages the estimates of several earlier target networks \citep{anschel2017averaged}. Rainbow
combined several such components and, in an ablation, found prioritised replay and multi-step learning to be the two
most important \citep{hessel2018rainbow}.

Each extension was, however, introduced with its own training recipe and evaluated on its own configuration. Deep
reinforcement learning results are sensitive to hyperparameters, random seeds and implementation
details \citep{henderson2018deep}, evaluation protocols differ between papers \citep{machado2018revisiting}, and
comparisons often rest on few runs and point estimates \citep{colas2018many,agarwal2021deep}. When an extension
outperforms DQN under one tuned configuration, it is therefore hard to say whether the gain belongs to the
extension or to the configuration.

We address this with a randomised controlled trial in the sense of \citet{pretorius2020dropout}. The
individuals of the trial are hyperparameter configurations sampled from a shared population, and every individual
receives every treatment. Each comparison with DQN is then paired on the same configuration and seed, which removes
the variation between configurations from the comparison while still averaging over the population. We use the
MinAtar suite \citep{young2019minatar}, whose small observations make 600 full training runs feasible on CPUs.

We ask two research questions. \emph{RQ1:} across a population of configurations and under one shared training
recipe, does each extension change sample efficiency, measured as the area under the evaluation curve (AUC),
relative to DQN? \emph{RQ2:} does a curve-based, population-level evaluation reach different conclusions from an
evaluation based on a single checkpoint? Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a paired trial of four DQN extensions on 30 sampled configurations and four MinAtar games (600 runs,
$3\times10^{9}$ frames), with code, raw results and provenance released\footnote{\url{https://github.com/2716924/research_project}};
\item evidence that Averaged DQN is the only extension that improves on DQN consistently, that the benefit of Double
DQN is small, and that PER and $n$-step returns are harmful under the shared recipe;
\item evidence that effects vary across the population: the learning rate is associated with larger differences
than almost all treatment effects, and the $n$-step deficit is concentrated at the lowest learning rate;
\item a demonstration that single-checkpoint endpoints confirm only some of the AUC findings.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews background and related work, Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the trial,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Deep Q-Networks}
An agent interacting with a Markov decision process observes a state $s_t$, takes an action $a_t$, receives a
reward $r_{t+1}$ and moves to $s_{t+1}$; it seeks to maximise the expected return discounted by
$\gamma\in[0,1)$ \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}. Q-learning \citep{watkins1992q} estimates the optimal action-value
function by bootstrapping from $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$. DQN \citep{mnih2015human} approximates $Q$ with a neural network
with parameters $\theta$, stores transitions in a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ \citep{lin1992self} and minimises
\begin{equation}
L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim U(\mathcal{D})}\big[(y-Q(s,a;\theta))^2\big]
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with the target
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-),
\label{eq:dqn}
\end{equation}
where $\theta^-$ are the parameters of a target network copied from $\theta$ every $C$ steps, and $y=r$ when $s'$
is terminal.

\subsection{The Four Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}
\emph{Double DQN} \citep{vanhasselt2016deep}. The maximum in~\eqref{eq:dqn} uses the same estimates to select and
to evaluate the next action, which biases the target upwards. Double DQN selects the action with the online network
and evaluates it with the target network:
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\,Q\big(s',\textstyle\arg\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta);\theta^-\big).
\end{equation}

\emph{Prioritised experience replay} \citep{schaul2016prioritized}. Instead of sampling uniformly, PER samples
transition $i$ with probability
\begin{equation}
P(i)=\frac{p_i^{\alpha}}{\sum_k p_k^{\alpha}},\qquad p_i=|\delta_i|+\epsilon,
\end{equation}
where $\delta_i$ is the latest temporal-difference (TD) error of the transition. New transitions receive the largest
priority seen so far. Importance-sampling weights $w_i=(|\mathcal{D}|\,P(i))^{-\beta}/\max_j w_j$ correct the bias
of non-uniform sampling, with $\beta$ annealed linearly to 1. For the proportional variant used here,
\citet{schaul2016prioritized} chose $\alpha=0.6$ and $\beta_0=0.4$ by a coarse grid search, and they reduced the step
size by a factor of four relative to their baseline because prioritised replay produces larger gradients.

\emph{Multi-step returns} \citep{hernandezgarcia2019understanding,sutton2018reinforcement}. The $n$-step target sums
$n$ observed rewards before bootstrapping:
\begin{equation}
y=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1}+\gamma^n\max_{a'}Q(s_{t+n},a';\theta^-).
\end{equation}
Without an off-policy correction, as in Rainbow \citep{hessel2018rainbow}, the reward sum follows the exploratory
behaviour policy rather than the greedy policy, which trades bias for faster propagation of reward.

\emph{Averaged DQN} \citep{anschel2017averaged}. Averaged DQN keeps the last $K$ target networks
$\theta^-_1,\dots,\theta^-_K$ and averages their action values before taking the maximum:
\begin{equation}
y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q(s',a';\theta^-_k).
\label{eq:avg}
\end{equation}
This reduces the variance of the target approximation error at the cost of $K$ forward passes per target, with the
same number of gradient updates as DQN \citep{anschel2017averaged}.

\subsection{Component Studies}
Rainbow's ablation on 57 Atari games found that removing prioritised replay or multi-step learning caused the
largest drops in median performance \citep{hessel2018rainbow}. \citet{obandoceron2021revisiting} repeated the
additive and ablative studies on smaller environments, including the five MinAtar games, and found multi-step returns
to be the component that most improved DQN on MinAtar. Their agents used the Adam optimiser \citep{kingma2015adam},
fixed hyperparameters and 10 runs per setting; they also observed on Atari that, with RMSProp, the Huber loss tends to
perform better than the mean squared error (MSE). \citet{fedus2020revisiting} found that uncorrected $n$-step returns
benefit uniquely from a larger replay capacity, whereas DQN does not. \citet{fujimoto2020equivalence} showed that a
loss evaluated on non-uniformly sampled data can be rewritten as a uniformly sampled loss with the same expected
gradient, which ties the effect of PER to the loss it is combined with.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
\citet{henderson2018deep} showed that hyperparameter settings, random seeds, environment properties and codebases
can each change reported performance substantially. \citet{machado2018revisiting} revisited the evaluation protocols
of the Arcade Learning Environment and introduced sticky actions to make it stochastic. \citet{colas2018many} related
the number of random seeds to the error rates of statistical tests, and \citet{agarwal2021deep} showed that
conclusions drawn from point estimates over few runs can differ substantially from those of a more thorough
statistical analysis, and advocated interval estimates. \citet{pretorius2020dropout} brought the randomised
controlled trial to deep learning: they sampled network designs from a population, trained identical copies of each
design under every intervention and tested the differences, over more than 12,000 trained networks. We apply this
design to DQN extensions and exploit the pairing explicitly.

\subsection{Trial Design}
Fig.~\ref{fig:design} summarises the design. The population $\Lambda$ is the Cartesian product of the candidate
values of six shared hyperparameters (Table~\ref{tab:population}). Each of the $N=30$ individuals $\lambda_j$ was
drawn by sampling every hyperparameter independently and uniformly from its candidates with a seeded generator, and
was given its own training seed $1337+j$. This is a simple random sample, and Table~\ref{tab:population} shows the
resulting counts, which are unbalanced for the replay buffer capacity. Every run filled the buffer with 5,000
transitions, collected by uniformly random actions, before learning began.

Five arms were trained on every individual: DQN, the control, and four treatments, each of which changes one
component (Section~\ref{sec:extensions}). The treatment-specific settings were fixed at published values rather
than sampled: $\alpha=0.6$, $\beta_0=0.4$ and $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ for PER \citep{schaul2016prioritized}, $n=3$ for
$n$-step DQN \citep{hessel2018rainbow} and $K=10$ for Averaged DQN \citep{anschel2017averaged}. Within an individual,
all arms share the configuration, the training seed and the evaluation seeds, so every comparison with DQN is paired.

The arms were trained on four MinAtar games \citep{young2019minatar}: Breakout, Asterix, Freeway and Space Invaders
(version~1, with the minimal action set). MinAtar's default settings were kept, including sticky actions with
probability 0.1 \citep{young2019minatar} and difficulty ramping in the games that support it. Seaquest was also run but
is excluded: in every arm in which it was run, a greedy evaluation episode exceeded the 100,000-step limit, because
MinAtar never ends a Seaquest episode in which the submarine stays on the surface, where it starts. The trial therefore comprises $4\times5\times30=600$
runs.

\subsection{Agent and Shared Training Recipe}
Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists the training settings. All arms use the network of the MinAtar DQN
baseline \citep{young2019minatar}: one convolutional layer with 16 $3\times3$ filters and stride 1, followed by fully
connected hidden layers of 128 units, with ReLU activations; whether there are one or two hidden layers is a sampled
hyperparameter. \citet{young2019minatar} chose 16 filters and 128 units as a quarter of the corresponding layers of
DQN \citep{mnih2015human}. Training uses RMSProp \citep{tieleman2012rmsprop} with the MSE
loss~\eqref{eq:loss} and one gradient update per environment step once learning has begun. Exploration is
$\varepsilon$-greedy, with $\varepsilon$ annealed linearly from 1.0 to 0.1 over the sampled duration. MinAtar has
no frame skipping, so a frame is one environment step, and each run lasts $5\times10^{6}$ frames, as in the
MinAtar baselines \citep{young2019minatar}.

The recipe is deliberately shared and is not the recipe of any one paper: DQN is usually trained with the Huber
loss \citep{obandoceron2021revisiting}, and \citet{schaul2016prioritized} lowered the step size for PER.
Changing one component under a fixed recipe is what isolates its effect, but it also means that the results concern
these components under this recipe.

The treatments were implemented as follows. PER stores priorities in a sum tree, draws each mini-batch by stratified
sampling over equal segments of the total priority, normalises the importance weights by the largest possible weight,
anneals $\beta$ linearly over the $5\times10^{6}$ frames and averages the weighted squared TD errors. $n$-step DQN
stores, for each transition, the discounted sum of the next three rewards and the state three steps later; at the
end of an episode, the remaining shorter returns are stored without a bootstrap. Averaged DQN stores a copy of the
target network at every target update and averages the last ten copies as in~\eqref{eq:avg}, or all of them while
fewer than ten exist.

\subsection{Evaluation and Primary Outcome}
Every $5\times10^{4}$ frames, the online network was evaluated greedily ($\varepsilon=0$) for 10 episodes, with the
same environment seeds (1000--1009) for every individual, arm and checkpoint. The mean return $G_i$ at checkpoint $i$
is one point on the evaluation curve, giving 100 points per run. An evaluation episode longer than $10^{5}$ steps
aborts the run without recording a score. The primary outcome is the trapezoidal area under the curve,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}=\sum_{i=1}^{99}\frac{G_i+G_{i+1}}{2}\,\Delta x,\qquad \Delta x=5\times10^{4}\ \text{frames},
\end{equation}
reported in units of $10^{6}$ (return $\times$ frames). The AUC rewards both fast learning and a high final level:
divided by the $4.95\times10^{6}$ frames it spans, it is the average evaluation return over training.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
The statistical unit is the individual; the ten evaluation episodes and the 100 checkpoints are repeated
measurements within a run, not replicates. For each game and treatment, we form the 30 paired differences
$d_j=\mathrm{AUC}_j^{\mathrm{treatment}}-\mathrm{AUC}_j^{\mathrm{DQN}}$ and test whether their mean is zero with a
two-sided paired $t$-test. We treat all $4\times4=16$ comparisons as one family and apply the Bonferroni
correction \citep{dunn1961multiple}, $\alpha=0.05/16=0.003125$, with matching simultaneous 99.69\% $t$-intervals. We
report each effect as the mean difference as a percentage of the DQN mean, with the standardised effect
$d_z=\bar d/s_d$ \citep{lakens2013calculating} and the number of individuals for which the treatment beat DQN.

This analysis departs from our research proposal in two ways. First, the proposal applied the Bonferroni
correction within each game ($\alpha=0.0125$); because our conclusions are drawn across games, we use the stricter
family-wide threshold. Second, the proposal would have replaced the $t$-test by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test
whenever a Shapiro--Wilk test \citep{shapiro1965analysis} rejected normality of the differences. Choosing the test
after a preliminary normality test alters the conditional error rates of the test that
follows \citep{rochon2012test}, so we instead report both tests for every comparison, with the $t$-test as primary
and the Wilcoxon test \citep{wilcoxon1945individual} as a sensitivity analysis. For reference, Shapiro--Wilk rejected
normality at the 0.05 level for 6 of the 16 comparisons.

Two secondary endpoints, analysed in the same way and treated as exploratory, address RQ2: the mean of the last ten
evaluations (the final $5\times10^{5}$ frames) and the final evaluation alone. The bands in the learning curves are
pointwise 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals over individuals, from 2,000 resamples \citep{efron1979bootstrap}.

\subsection{Implementation and Data Provenance}
The agents were implemented in PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} (version 2.13) with the
Gymnasium \citep{towers2024gymnasium} interface to MinAtar (version 1.0.15) and trained on CPUs, with four threads
per job, on the MSL cluster at the University of the Witwatersrand. The code includes unit tests and analytic checks
of every arm's target and replay computations, which run before each cluster job.

An initial run used a PER implementation that applied the priority exponent twice when inserting a new transition,
giving it priority $p_{\max}^{\alpha^2}$ instead of $p_{\max}^{\alpha}$. After the fix, the PER, Double DQN,
$n$-step and Averaged DQN arms were rerun with the corrected code. The three arms that do not use prioritised replay
reproduced all 348 of their overlapping learning curves exactly, which shows that training is reproducible and that
the fix affected only PER. The DQN arm, which was not rerun, is therefore taken from the initial run, as are
$n$-step individuals 18--29 in Freeway, whose rerun job failed to start. Every stored AUC was recomputed from its
curve, every individual was checked to have the same configuration and seed in every arm, and all tests were
reproduced by an independent script.

\subsection{Primary Outcome}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:effects} report the 16 comparisons, nine of which are significant at the
family-wide threshold. Averaged DQN had a higher AUC than DQN in Asterix ($+57.5\%$), Freeway ($+20.9\%$) and Space
Invaders ($+19.5\%$), and beat DQN on 25, 24 and 26 of the 30 individuals. Double DQN was better only in Space
Invaders ($+11.4\%$, 21 of 30 individuals); its differences in Asterix ($+28.2\%$, $p_t=0.048$) and Freeway
($-8.5\%$, $p_t=0.014$) were not significant. PER was worse in Freeway ($-35.3\%$) and Space Invaders ($-50.0\%$),
beating DQN on only 3 and 1 individuals. $n$-step DQN was worse in Asterix ($-80.0\%$), Freeway ($-59.4\%$) and Space
Invaders ($-21.0\%$), with 2, 0 and 6 wins. The significant effects have $|d_z|$ between 0.59 and 1.64.

In Breakout no treatment differed significantly from DQN. The point estimates lie between $+0.1\%$ and $+4.5\%$, but
the intervals extend from $-10.8\%$ to $+17.3\%$, so the data do not show that the extensions are equivalent to DQN
there either.

\subsection{Learning Dynamics}
The learning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}) show how these differences arise. In Breakout the curves rise quickly
and then overlap for the rest of training, ending between 13.2 and 14.8 (mean of the last ten evaluations); $n$-step
DQN learnt fastest early on, with a mean return of 14.0 against 12.0 for DQN at $5\times10^{5}$ frames. In Asterix,
$n$-step DQN barely improved: its mean curve never exceeded 1.24, whereas DQN ended at 3.8. In Freeway, Averaged DQN
led from early in training (31.6 against 20.1 for DQN at $5\times10^{5}$ frames) and ended highest (44.1 against
38.4). In Space Invaders, PER started level with DQN (23.6 against 23.5 at the first evaluation), fell to 13.0 by
$10^{6}$ frames and ended at about half the level of DQN (19.1 against 35.9).

\subsection{Variation Across Configurations}
Performance varied widely between configurations (Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}). For DQN, the standard deviation of
the AUC across individuals ranged from 26\% (Breakout) to 84\% (Asterix) of its mean. The paired design removes this
variation from the comparisons.

Much of this variation was associated with the learning rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:lr}). The mean AUC of DQN at a learning rate
of $10^{-4}$ was 1.7 to 5.9 times its mean at $10^{-3}$ (Breakout 86.5 against 52.2, Asterix 35.1 against 6.0,
Freeway 205.4 against 113.2, Space Invaders 287.9 against 90.8). As a ratio of means, this spread exceeded the
treatment effect in 15 of the 16 comparisons; the exception was $n$-step DQN in Freeway. The lowest learning rate gave
the highest mean AUC for every arm in every game except $n$-step DQN in Asterix and Freeway.

The $n$-step deficit depended strongly on the learning rate. In Freeway, its mean AUC at $10^{-4}$ was 16.1, against
205.4 for DQN, a ratio of 0.08, compared with ratios of 0.50 and 0.63 at the two larger rates; Asterix showed the
same pattern (0.09, 0.19 and 0.70). PER, by contrast, stayed below DQN at every learning rate in Freeway and Space
Invaders, with ratios between 0.41 and 0.74 and no consistent trend.

The nine findings survive several checks. Under the per-game threshold of our proposal ($\alpha=0.0125$), the same
nine comparisons are significant. The Wilcoxon test confirms eight of them at the family-wide threshold; the
exception is Double DQN in Space Invaders ($p_W=0.012$), whose interval also only just excludes zero
($[+0.1\%, +22.6\%]$), so this finding rests on the $t$-test alone. Restricting $n$-step DQN in Freeway to the
individuals of the rerun (0--17) gives $-53.3\%$ ($p_t=2.4\times10^{-5}$), in line with the full sample.

The secondary endpoints (Table~\ref{tab:robust}) agree in direction with all nine findings, but they are noisier.
With the mean of the last ten evaluations, five of the nine remain significant at the family-wide threshold; with the
final evaluation alone, only three do. No comparison that was not significant on the AUC became significant on
either secondary endpoint.

\subsection{Effect of the PER Fix}
Fixing the priority bug did not change PER significantly in any game. Comparing the same 30 individuals before and
after the fix, the AUC of PER changed by $+2.1\%$ in Breakout ($p_t=0.58$), $-0.9\%$ in Asterix ($p_t=0.89$),
$-9.4\%$ in Freeway ($p_t=0.27$) and $+4.2\%$ in Space Invaders ($p_t=0.43$), without any correction for multiple
comparisons. Before the fix, PER was already $28.6\%$ and $52.0\%$ below DQN in Freeway and Space Invaders, both
significant at the family-wide threshold, and did not differ significantly from DQN in Breakout or Asterix. The
deficit of PER is therefore not an artefact of the bug.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Which Extensions Help?}
Averaged DQN was the only extension that improved on DQN reliably. It was better in three of the four games and on at
least 24 of 30 individuals in each; the Wilcoxon test confirmed all three results, and all three kept their direction
under both secondary endpoints. This is consistent with its motivation, the reduction of variance in the
target \citep{anschel2017averaged}. The gain has a computational cost, since each target needs ten forward passes
rather than one.

Double DQN helped little. Its one significant result, in Space Invaders, was the weakest of the nine findings, and
its other estimates had mixed signs. Under this recipe, decoupling action selection from evaluation was not an
important source of improvement on MinAtar.

PER and $n$-step returns were harmful, which contrasts with their importance in the Rainbow ablation on
Atari \citep{hessel2018rainbow} and, for $n$-step returns, with the MinAtar results of
\citet{obandoceron2021revisiting}. Two differences between the studies may explain this, although our data cannot
separate them. The first is the recipe. \citet{schaul2016prioritized} reduced the step size for PER, and Obando-Ceron
and Castro used Adam with one update every four steps. We used
RMSProp with the MSE loss and one update per step, and with RMSProp they found that the Huber loss tended to work
better than the MSE on Atari. Because non-uniform sampling has the same expected gradient as a different loss under
uniform sampling \citep{fujimoto2020equivalence}, PER combined with the MSE may weight large errors more heavily than
intended. The second difference is the population. In Asterix and Freeway, the $n$-step deficit was concentrated at
the lowest learning rate, which was the best learning rate for DQN, and was much smaller at $10^{-3}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lr}). A study that tunes each method separately evaluates each at a single point of such a curve. We
found no consistent association with the replay buffer capacity, the factor that \citet{fedus2020revisiting}
identified for $n$-step returns, but the subgroups are small.

In Breakout, all arms learnt quickly to a similar level. Every interval there includes zero, but their width means
that small effects in either direction cannot be ruled out.

\subsection{RQ2: What Does a Population-Level Evaluation Change?}
Our results answer RQ2 in three ways. First, the endpoint matters. The final evaluation alone confirmed three of the
nine AUC findings and the mean of the last ten evaluations confirmed five, although all nine kept their direction;
an evaluation based on a single checkpoint would have missed most of the effects. Second, the configuration matters at least
as much as the algorithm. The learning rate alone was associated with larger differences than 15 of the 16 treatment
effects. Even Averaged DQN, the most consistent improvement, failed to beat DQN on 5, 6 and 4 of the 30
configurations in Asterix, Freeway and Space Invaders, so a comparison on one randomly chosen configuration would
have favoured DQN with a probability of 0.13 to 0.20. Third, the paired design makes these effects detectable despite this variation: the significant effects have
$|d_z|$ between 0.59 and 1.64, whereas the spread of the AUC between individuals is often as large as the differences
between arms (Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}).

Our proposal hypothesised that isolated effects would be smaller than those reported in the literature. This was
partly supported: the benefits reported for PER and $n$-step returns did not appear under the shared recipe, whereas
those of Averaged DQN did.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
Each configuration was trained with one seed, so within an individual the effects of the configuration and the seed
cannot be separated; both are shared across arms, so the comparisons remain paired. The results hold for one shared
recipe with treatment settings fixed at published values; tuning each arm separately could change them. The trial
covers four MinAtar games and $5\times10^{6}$ frames, so longer training or other environments could give different
results, and Seaquest could not be evaluated under the present protocol. The AUC combines learning speed and final
level over the observed interval. The 30 configurations are a simple random sample, so the subgroup analyses are
descriptive. Finally, the DQN arm comes from the initial run; this is justified by the exact reproduction of every
rerun curve, but it is a departure from running all arms together.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
In a paired randomised controlled trial of four DQN extensions over 30 sampled configurations and four MinAtar games,
only Averaged DQN improved on DQN consistently. Double DQN helped only slightly, and prioritised replay and multi-step
returns were harmful under the shared recipe, contrary to earlier component studies. Differences between
configurations, especially in the learning rate, were larger than most treatment effects, and single-checkpoint
endpoints confirmed few of the findings. Future work should add several seeds per configuration, compare recipes
tuned for each arm, examine the training dynamics of PER and $n$-step returns directly, and evaluate Seaquest with a
capped evaluation protocol.
